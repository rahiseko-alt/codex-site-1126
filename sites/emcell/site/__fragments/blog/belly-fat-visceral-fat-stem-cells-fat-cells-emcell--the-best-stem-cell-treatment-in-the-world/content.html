Scientists Reveal the Hidden Role of “Middle-Age” Fat Cells

The Surprising Source of Age-Related Belly Fat

Have you ever wondered why belly fat tends to grow with age—even if your weight stays roughly the same? 

American scientists have finally uncovered the biological reason: new types of fat-producing stem cells awaken in middle age.

According to a study published in Science and reported by Neuroscience News, these so-called CP-A cells become active later in life and generate fat tissue with remarkable intensity, especially in the abdominal area.

Why Fat Around the Waist Is More Than a Cosmetic Issue 

Gaining fat around the waist after the age of 40 is not only a cosmetic concern but also a serious metabolic issue.

Visceral fat (the kind that surrounds your internal organs):

•slows down metabolism;

•accelerates aging;

•increases the risk of diabetes, heart disease, and even cancer.

So, understanding why belly fat grows with age isn’t just interesting — it’s vital for long-term health.

The Role of CP-A Cells: Aging Switches Them On

Researchers from the City of Hope National Medical Center, a leading U.S. institute in cancer and diabetes research, made a groundbreaking discovery.

“We discovered that aging triggers the emergence of a unique type of stem cell called CP-A, which generates fat with remarkable intensity—especially around the abdomen in middle age,” says Dr. Qiong Wang.

Unlike typical young stem cells, which lose their ability to divide as we age, adipocyte precursor cells (APCs) in older individuals become more active instead of slowing down.

To test their theory, scientists transplanted fat precursor cells from both young and old mice into healthy young mice. The results were stunning:

APCs from older mice rapidly produced new fat cells—even inside a young body.

This proves that the age of the cell, not the host, determines its fat-producing potential.

A Hidden Switch: The LIFR Pathway

Digging deeper, scientists used single-cell RNA sequencing to observe the gene activity of these cells. They found that in young tissue, CP-A cells are nearly dormant — but in middle age, they become highly active.

What’s behind this switch? The researchers identified a key player:

LIFR (leukemia inhibitory factor receptor) — a molecular signaling pathway that activates in midlife.

“We’ve shown that the LIFR pathway activates CP-A cells and stimulates them to produce large numbers of new fat cells,” explains Dr. Wang.

Humans Are No Exception: The Same Mechanism Found in People

To confirm their findings, the scientists studied human tissues collected from people of different ages and observed a similar pattern: in middle age, the number of CP-A cells in white fat tissue increases. This means the mechansim found in mice also applies to humans.

This discovery sheds new light on why fat tends to accumulate in the abdomen as we age — and not just due to lifestyle or slowing metabolism.
