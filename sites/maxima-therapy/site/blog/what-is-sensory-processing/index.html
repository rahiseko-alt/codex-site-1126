What is sensory processing?

Some little ones find a busy store, a scratchy tag or a toothbrush really hard to handle. Here's what sensory processing is and which signs are worth noticing.

In this article

  • How our senses work together
  • What it can feel like
  • Signs worth noticing
  • If this sounds familiar
Maxima Therapy TeamEarly Intervention program

How our senses work together

We learn about the world through our senses: what we see and hear, what we touch, and how our body moves and feels gravity. Most of the time all of this works together without us noticing. The brain takes in the information, connects it, and helps us understand what's going on and how to respond.

Some children take in and use this information differently. Their nervous system reacts in its own way, so the picture they get of their body and their surroundings isn't always clear or reliable. You may hear this called a sensory integration or sensory processing difference.

For some kids, it's as if the conductor has lost control. Each section plays at its own volume and its own pace, and the music stops making sense.

What it can feel like

For a child with strong sensory differences, a simple trip to the supermarket can feel like a loud rock concert:

  • Lights

    Bright store lights can seem to flicker and buzz.

  • Sounds

    A squeaky shopping cart can sound as loud as thunder.

  • Smells

    The meat section can smell like a garbage dump.

  • Crowds

    Moving through aisles full of shoppers can feel like a bumper car ride.

Things most adults barely notice can be too much for a child whose senses work differently.

Signs worth noticing

  • Reactions to the senses

    Reacts too much or too little to touch, sounds, sights, movement, tastes or smells. Has a very high or a very low pain threshold.

  • Movement and balance

    Avoids swings, slides and jumping, or looks for them all the time. Gets dizzy very easily, or never gets dizzy. Seems clumsy or careless.

  • Everyday routines

    Is bothered by certain fabrics, tags or waistbands. Fights tooth brushing or hair washing. Avoids foods most kids enjoy.

  • Attention and groups

    Often "tunes out" or "acts up". Has a hard time staying with one toy or activity. Gets upset in busy groups, like a playgroup or a family party.

Good to know

If your child often squints, blinks or rubs their eyes, it may be a sign of a vision problem that hasn't been found yet. It's worth mentioning to your child's doctor.

If this sounds familiar

One or two of these signs on their own don't mean something is wrong. Every child has things they love and things they can't stand. If several signs sound familiar and they get in the way of everyday life, talk to your child's doctor or a pediatric therapist.
